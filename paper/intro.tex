\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) have made large-scale pretraining the dominant recipe for robot
manipulation~\citep{rt2, openvla, pi0}. Trained on large, diverse robot datasets, the vision-language
backbone provides priors strong enough that a new task rarely has to be learned from scratch, and in
practice a VLA is deployed by fine-tuning it on demonstrations of the target task.

The limitation of this recipe lies in the data it imitates. Teleoperated demonstrations are inherently
suboptimal, interleaving expert behavior with hesitation, detours, and outright failure, and their
quality varies even within a single trajectory~\citep{grrl, pistar06}. Collecting more of them does not
change this, since an imitation objective treats every transition as an equally valid target. Filtering
the data away discards exactly the scarce experience the recipe depends on, and refining the policy
through online rollouts is costly and unsafe at this scale.

Can an optimal policy be extracted from suboptimal human data with a VLA-scale model?
Temporal-difference (TD) learning and offline reinforcement learning (RL) promise precisely this,
stitching high-value segments of otherwise mediocre trajectories into behavior that no single
demonstration exhibits~\citep{cql, iql}. A VLA, however, is not the policy for which this machinery was
designed: it predicts a fixed, long action chunk~\citep{act, pi0}, it typically tackles long-horizon
tasks where standard one-step TD struggles~\citep{qc}, and its heavy backbone cannot be trained jointly
with a critic at practical cost.

These three frictions map onto the three stages of any offline RL pipeline, and each stage has to be
rethought. The fixed chunk raises a question of granularity: what should the critic score, and for how
long should the robot commit? Critics that value every prefix of an action sequence
exist~\citep{acsac, aqc}, but at VLA scale the commitment has so far been fixed in
advance~\citep{corft, deas}. The long horizon and the frozen backbone raise a question of stability:
TD over chunks amplifies overestimation exactly where data is thin~\citep{dqc}, and the critic must
learn on representations it cannot shape. Conservative chunk-level targets exist as
well~\citep{iql, deas}, but which ingredients actually matter at this scale has never been isolated.
And solving both exposes a question the pipeline usually hides: a learned value does not improve a
robot by existing. It must act on the policy through some mechanism, by rescoring sampled
chunks~\citep{vgps, robomonkey}, by deciding how long to commit~\citep{aqc}, or by retraining the
policy~\citep{parl}. These mechanisms are routinely assumed and rarely compared, and, as we show, they
are far from equivalent~\citep{emaq, park2024bottleneck}.

Our contribution is accordingly not a new component but a working composition, determined by
measurement. We attach a prefix-valued causal-transformer critic~\citep{acsac} to the VLA's frozen
representations, train it with conservative in-sample chunk-level targets in the spirit
of~\citet{deas}, and put the value to work in the two places where controlled comparisons show it
helps: deciding, state by state, how much of each predicted chunk to execute, and updating the policy
itself. Test-time rescoring, the mechanism most prior work presumes, is the one our experiments rule
out. The resulting pipeline surpasses supervised fine-tuning from the very same demonstrations,
without a single additional rollout.

Prior efforts toward this goal largely sidestep value learning. Advantage-conditioned fine-tuning
\citep{pistar06}, advantage-weighted imitation~\citep{arfm, arm}, return conditioning~\citep{reinbot},
and preference tuning~\citep{nora15} reshape the imitation objective with outcome signals; they
inherit the stability of supervised learning together with its defining limit, since the policy is
never trained to exceed the behavior it imitates. The few direct attempts each stop short of a
complete path. Q-Transformer~\citep{qtransformer} predates chunked policies; CO-RFT~\citep{corft}
extends TD to action chunks but commits to fixed-length execution at small scale; GR-RL~\citep{grrl}
learns a value yet uses it only to filter data; DEAS~\citep{deas} learns a strong chunk-level value
but deploys it by rescoring sampled chunks, a mechanism whose improvement is bounded by the proposals
it ranks~\citep{emaq}. Where prior work incorporates chunking as a feature, we treat the chunk as the
source of the problem structure: it dictates the credit granularity, the stability recipe, and the
space of deployment mechanisms alike.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i)~We present a complete offline reinforcement fine-tuning pipeline for chunked VLAs, from a critic
on frozen representations through adaptive commitment to policy extraction, requiring no online
interaction. (ii)~We compare, under a single learned value, the mechanisms by which it can act on a
chunked policy, and show that test-time rescoring is structurally capped while adaptive commitment and
policy extraction are not. (iii)~We provide a component-isolated stability recipe for
chunk-space TD at VLA scale, identifying what is decisive and what silently fails. (iv)~We validate
the pipeline end to end on standard offline RL benchmarks~\citep{ogbench} and VLA-scale
manipulation~\citep{robocasa}, using only the demonstrations that supervised fine-tuning already uses.
